    CodeCartographer →

    Static analysis for Python codebases: dependency graphs, circular-import detection via Tarjan's strongly connected components, a static call graph, and dead-code detection.

    Graph algorithms implemented from scratch, no runtime dependencies. Outputs a self-contained interactive HTML map you can open in any browser.

    PythonASTgraph algorithmszero deps

    Each node is a module, each arrow an import. Tarjan's algorithm walks the graph depth-first, stamping every node with the order it was discovered (index) and the lowest index reachable from its subtree (lowlink). A node whose lowlink comes back equal to its own index is the root of a strongly connected component: everything above it on the stack imports its way back round to it. Components larger than one node are circular imports. It is one pass, O(V+E), which is why the real tool can run it on a whole codebase without you noticing.

    HarmonicDNA →

    Applies Smith-Waterman local alignment, the bioinformatics algorithm for comparing DNA sequences, to chord progressions extracted from audio.

    Two songs in, out comes a similarity score and a visual alignment of the passages that are most harmonically alike.

    Pythonsignal processingsequence alignment

    synthesised, nothing plays on its own

    Every number is computed by the same rules the project uses: H[i,j] = max(0, diagonal + s, up + gap, left + gap), with s scoring the two chords by harmonic relationship rather than by whether the labels are equal, and gap = -0.5. The named pieces are public domain and appear here as harmonic reductions, not transcriptions: the detector matches audio against 24 major and minor templates, so sevenths, inversions and passing chords collapse before the aligner sees them. That loss is the method, not a shortcut taken for the demo. Where a tune is played it is there so you can recognise the piece; the alignment above is computed from the chords alone, which is exactly why Twinkle Twinkle and the alphabet song come out identical.

    EchoLock → ongoing

    A voice-and-passphrase unlock overlay. The MFCC speaker-verification pipeline is implemented from scratch on numpy.

    Paired with a passphrase that rotates daily and is checked by an offline speech recognizer. No pretrained speaker model and no cloud calls. The README documents the measured tradeoffs behind the scoring function and threshold.

    Pythonnumpyspeaker verification

    nothing is uploaded or kept; the mic is released after each take
    Say the passphrase 2.0s

    Voice enrolled. Press Try to unlock and say it again.

    The same pipeline the project runs, in the browser: the signal is framed and windowed, an FFT gives the magnitude spectrum, a bank of 26 triangular filters spaced on the mel scale sums it into bands, the log is taken, and a DCT decorrelates those bands into cepstral coefficients. Coefficients 1 to 12 are the voiceprint. C0 is dropped because it is loudness, not identity, which is what stops the check from being fooled by someone simply speaking louder. Verification is cosine similarity against the enrolled vector, after mean and variance normalisation. Notice that the pitch slider barely moves the score while the vocal-tract slider moves it a long way: MFCCs describe the shape of the tract doing the filtering, not the vocal folds driving it, so singing the passphrase higher does not lock you out. Raise the threshold past 0.98 and you start rejecting the owner over a phone line; lower it and the flatmate gets in. That line is a tradeoff someone has to pick, not a setting with a correct value. Enrol your own voice if you would rather test it against yourself than against a synthesised speaker: the samples stay in this tab, nothing is uploaded or written to disk, the microphone is released the moment each take ends, and Forget my voice drops the template.
